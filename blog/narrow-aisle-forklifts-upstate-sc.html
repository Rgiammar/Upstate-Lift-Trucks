Narrow Aisle Forklifts
for Upstate SC
Warehouses

July 2026
9 min read
Greenville · Spartanburg · Anderson SC

The fastest way to increase storage capacity in an Upstate SC warehouse without expanding the building footprint is to go narrower on aisles and taller on racking. Modern distribution facilities near the SC Inland Port in Greer and along the Woodruff Road corridor are doing exactly that - replacing 12 to 14-foot counterbalance aisles with 8 to 10-foot narrow aisle configurations and adding rack height to reclaim cubic footage that previously went unused. But narrower aisles require different equipment, and choosing the wrong forklift class for your aisle width is an expensive mistake. This guide covers the narrow aisle equipment options relevant to Upstate SC warehouse operations and how to match the right equipment to your specific configuration.

Why Narrow Aisle Matters for Upstate SC Operations

Warehouse real estate in the Greenville-Spartanburg market has tightened significantly over the past several years. The SC Inland Port's continued growth, expanding e-commerce distribution, and the automotive supplier base's demand for nearby finished goods and component storage have driven industrial lease rates higher. Building expansions are expensive and often constrained by site limitations. Going narrow is frequently the most cost-effective way to add storage positions within an existing footprint.

The math is compelling. A 100,000 square foot distribution facility operating with 12-foot counterbalance aisles might achieve 18,000 to 22,000 pallet positions. Converting to 8-foot narrow aisles with the same footprint can push that to 26,000 to 32,000 positions - a 40 to 50 percent increase in storage capacity without adding a single square foot of building. That is significant when industrial rents in the Greenville market are running $8 to $12 per square foot annually.

Understanding Aisle Width Categories

The forklift industry uses three aisle width categories, each defining which equipment class is appropriate and what storage density is achievable.

CategoryAisle WidthEquipmentTypical Application
Wide Aisle11 to 13+ feetCounterbalance forkliftsGeneral warehousing, manufacturing, receiving
Narrow Aisle (NA)8 to 10 feetReach trucks, swing-reach trucksHigh-bay distribution, 3PL, storage-intensive operations
Very Narrow Aisle (VNA)5 to 6 feetTurret trucks, order pickersMaximum density storage, automated or semi-automated warehouses
Aisle Width Must Be Decided Before Racking Is Installed The aisle width category determines the racking layout, the floor flatness specification, and the equipment class - and these decisions are interdependent. Changing aisle width after racking is installed requires a complete racking redesign and re-installation. For any Upstate SC operation planning a new facility or a racking reconfiguration, the equipment decision and the racking layout need to be developed together, not sequentially.

Narrow Aisle Equipment Types

01
Stand-Up Reach Truck
The workhorse of narrow aisle warehousing - the most common narrow aisle unit in Upstate SC distribution

A reach truck is a stand-up electric forklift with a pantograph or scissors mechanism that extends the forks forward to reach into racking, then retracts to bring the load back over the truck's wheelbase before traveling. This design allows the truck to operate in aisles as narrow as 8 feet while lifting loads to heights of 30 feet or more - capabilities that a counterbalance forklift cannot match at that aisle width.

Reach trucks are the most common narrow aisle unit in Upstate SC distribution environments. The 3PL facilities and distribution centers near the SC Inland Port in Greer that run high-bay racking above 20 feet rely on reach trucks as their primary storage unit. The Woodruff Road corridor warehouses serving retail and e-commerce distribution show the same pattern.

Reach trucks are electric-only and strictly indoor equipment. They are not suitable for outdoor use, uneven surfaces, or loading dock approaches - a counterbalance unit handles those tasks while the reach truck handles the high-bay storage aisles. Most narrow aisle operations run a mixed fleet of reach trucks and counterbalance units for exactly this reason.

02
Double Deep Reach Truck
Extended reach mechanism for storing two pallets deep per aisle face - increases density without narrowing aisles further

A double deep reach truck uses an extended pantograph that can reach two pallet positions deep into the racking, allowing pallets to be stored two rows deep rather than one. This effectively doubles the storage positions accessible from a single aisle face without reducing aisle width further. The tradeoff is LIFO inventory management - the front pallet must be moved to access the rear one - which makes double deep unsuitable for operations with high SKU diversity or FIFO inventory requirements.

Double deep configurations work well for operations with a smaller number of high-volume SKUs where storing multiple pallets of the same product two-deep is practical. Beverage distribution, raw material staging in manufacturing, and seasonal goods storage are examples where double deep reach trucks provide meaningful density gains without inventory management complications.

03
Order Picker
Elevates the operator with the load for case and each-level picking in narrow aisle environments

An order picker raises the operator platform along with the forks, allowing the operator to pick cases or individual items from racking at height rather than picking from ground level only. Order pickers operate in narrow aisles (8 to 10 feet) and are essential equipment for e-commerce fulfillment, retail distribution, and any operation with case-level or each-level picking requirements at multiple rack levels.

The growing e-commerce and retail distribution presence in the Upstate SC market - particularly facilities near the SC Inland Port in Greer serving import distribution chains - has driven demand for order pickers significantly. These are electric-only units with lower lift heights than reach trucks (typically up to 30 feet) but are specifically designed for the picking workflow rather than pallet-in pallet-out storage.

Fall protection is an OSHA requirement for order pickers - operators must be secured to the platform with a restraint whenever the platform is elevated. This is one of the most commonly cited violations in order picker operations and should be part of operator training and pre-shift inspection protocols.

04
Turret Truck (VNA)
Rotating mast or forks for very narrow aisle operation - maximum storage density, specialized floor requirements

A turret truck operates in very narrow aisles of 5 to 6 feet - aisles so narrow the truck cannot turn within them. Instead of turning, the forks or mast rotate to serve racking on both sides of the aisle from a single straight travel path. Turret trucks achieve the maximum possible storage density from a given footprint and can lift to heights of 40 feet or more, but they require extremely flat, smooth floor surfaces (typically F-number 50 or above) and are generally guided by rail or wire guidance systems embedded in the floor.

VNA operations are less common in the Upstate SC market than standard narrow aisle reach truck configurations, but they appear in the most storage-intensive distribution operations - particularly facilities that have maximized their footprint and need to extract every possible pallet position from the building cube. The floor specification requirement is a significant capital investment that makes VNA conversion a major facility project rather than simply an equipment change.

05
Swing-Reach Truck
Combines reach truck and turret truck capabilities - serves both narrow aisle storage and cross-aisle travel

A swing-reach truck (sometimes called a swingmast or sideloader-style reach truck) combines a rotating mast with the ability to travel in wider cross-aisles, unlike a pure turret truck that is constrained to the storage aisles. This makes swing-reach trucks a practical middle ground for operations that need narrower aisles than a standard reach truck requires but cannot commit to the floor specifications and guidance systems of a full VNA turret truck operation.

Swing-reach trucks operate in aisles as narrow as 6 to 7 feet while still being able to travel freely in cross-aisles and staging areas. They are more expensive than standard reach trucks and require operator training for the rotational mast function, but they offer a genuine density improvement over standard narrow aisle reach truck configurations without the full VNA infrastructure commitment.

Floor Flatness Requirements

Floor flatness is the most frequently underestimated constraint in narrow aisle operations. As aisles get narrower and lift heights increase, floor flatness tolerance becomes critical to safe operation and equipment longevity.

Standard warehouse concrete floors are typically specified to F-number 25 to 35 (FF/FL), which is adequate for counterbalance forklifts. Narrow aisle reach truck operations should target F-number 40 to 50. VNA turret truck operations require F-number 50 or higher - a significantly more demanding specification that most existing warehouse floors do not meet without grinding or resurfacing.

For Upstate SC operations converting an existing facility to narrow aisle, a floor flatness survey should be completed before equipment is specified. In older industrial buildings throughout Greenville and Spartanburg counties, floor flatness can be a significant conversion cost that changes the financial case for narrow aisle equipment.

Racking Compatibility

Narrow aisle reach trucks require selective racking with specific beam and upright configurations that create clear pathways for the extending forks. The racking manufacturer and the forklift provider both need to be involved in the design process to confirm that the racking beam configuration, upright spacing, and floor-level clearance all match the reach truck's fork extension envelope.

This is not a step to skip. A reach truck that cannot fully extend its forks to the designed rack depth due to upright interference is an operational problem that requires either racking modification or equipment replacement to fix - neither of which is inexpensive after the racking is installed.

Narrow Aisle in the Upstate SC Market

The facilities most actively deploying narrow aisle equipment in the Greenville-Spartanburg market fall into three categories:

  • 3PL and distribution centers near the SC Inland Port in Greer. The intermodal volume flowing through Greer supports a cluster of high-throughput distribution operations that need maximum storage density in facilities serving multiple customers. Reach trucks in 8 to 10-foot aisles with 30-foot rack heights are the standard configuration in this corridor.
  • E-commerce and retail fulfillment operations. The growth of direct-to-consumer and retail replenishment distribution in Greenville County has driven demand for order picker operations alongside reach truck storage. These facilities typically run reach trucks for pallet putaway and retrieval and order pickers for case and each-level fulfillment.
  • Cold storage and food distribution. Cold storage real estate is expensive to build and operate. Narrow aisle configurations are near-universal in cold storage because the density gain directly reduces the cubic footage that needs to be refrigerated. Cold-rated reach trucks and order pickers are standard equipment in the cold chain distribution operations serving the Upstate SC food industry.

Choosing the Right Narrow Aisle Configuration

Work through these questions before committing to a narrow aisle configuration:

  • What is your current or planned aisle width? This determines which equipment class is viable. Existing buildings with fixed column spacing may constrain your aisle options.
  • What is your maximum rack height? Reach trucks top out around 35 feet. VNA turret trucks can reach 45 feet or more. If you are targeting heights above 35 feet, standard reach trucks are not the answer.
  • What is your floor flatness? Get a survey before specifying equipment. Floor conditions may limit your options or add conversion cost to your project budget.
  • Is your operation pallet-in pallet-out, or do you pick cases and eaches? Pallet storage only: reach trucks. Case and each picking at height: add order pickers. Both: a mixed fleet of reach trucks and order pickers.
  • What is your SKU count and inventory turnover? High SKU count with FIFO requirements points toward standard single-deep reach truck racking. Low SKU count, high volume per SKU may support double deep reach truck configurations.
  • Are you in a new building or converting an existing one? New construction can be designed for the target aisle width and floor specification from the start. Existing building conversions need column spacing and floor flatness assessed before committing to a configuration.
