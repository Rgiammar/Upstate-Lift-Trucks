Forklift Attachments
Guide for Upstate SC
Operations

July 2026
9 min read
Greenville · Spartanburg · Anderson SC

Standard forks handle the majority of pallet work in most Upstate SC facilities without issue. But when your loads do not fit the standard pallet profile - cylinders, bales, drums, slip sheets, oversized cartons, or fragile stacked goods - an attachment can eliminate a manual handling step, reduce damage, improve throughput, and in some cases make a job that previously required two people and extra equipment possible with one operator and one forklift. This guide covers the most common forklift attachments used in Greenville, Spartanburg, and Anderson SC operations and how to choose the right one for your application.

The First Thing to Understand: Attachments Reduce Capacity

Before selecting any attachment, the capacity impact needs to be understood and respected. Every attachment adds weight to the front of the truck and shifts the load center forward, both of which reduce the forklift's effective lifting capacity. This is not a minor effect - a heavy attachment on a 5,000 lb forklift can reduce working capacity to 3,500 lbs or less depending on the attachment weight and load center distance.

OSHA requires that a new nameplate be affixed to the forklift whenever an attachment is installed, showing the revised capacity with the attachment in place. Operating a forklift with an attachment using the unmodified capacity rating is a safety violation and a real risk - attachments are involved in a disproportionate share of tip-over incidents precisely because operators underestimate the capacity change.

Capacity Derating - Always Verify When evaluating any attachment, get the attachment weight from the manufacturer and ask your forklift provider to calculate the derated capacity at your typical load center. Do not estimate this. A 400 lb sideshifter on a 5,000 lb forklift lifting at a 24-inch load center may reduce capacity to 4,200 lbs - but the same attachment lifting at a 36-inch load center may reduce it further. The math matters and the nameplate must be updated before the attachment goes into service.

Common Forklift Attachments for Upstate SC Operations

01
Sideshifter
Lateral fork movement without repositioning the truck - the most widely used attachment in warehouse environments

A sideshifter allows the operator to move the forks left or right hydraulically without moving the truck. This eliminates the need to reposition for precise pallet placement in racking, reduces wear on rack uprights from repeated minor adjustments, and speeds up cycle times significantly in high-throughput environments. On many modern forklifts sideshifters come standard from the factory, but on older units or simpler configurations they may need to be added.

In the Upstate SC distribution and 3PL environment, sideshifters are effectively mandatory equipment for any unit working in rack aisles. The precision improvement alone pays for the attachment quickly in reduced rack damage and faster operator cycle times. For automotive supplier facilities doing precision parts placement, sideshifters are equally valuable.

Capacity ImpactMinor - 100 to 300 lbs typically
Cost Range$800 to $2,500
Common OnMost counterbalance and reach trucks
Best For Any operation working in racking. Near-universal in warehouse and distribution environments. Should be standard on virtually every unit working in a rack aisle in the Greenville-Spartanburg market.
02
Fork Positioner
Hydraulic fork width adjustment from the operator position - eliminates manual fork repositioning between loads

A fork positioner allows the operator to adjust the distance between forks hydraulically without leaving the seat. This is particularly valuable in operations that handle multiple pallet sizes - a facility receiving both standard GMA 48x40 pallets and smaller 36x36 export pallets needs to reposition forks frequently, and doing that manually adds time and creates ergonomic risk for the operator. Fork positioners also improve consistency by allowing operators to set the correct fork spacing precisely every time.

In Upstate SC manufacturing facilities that receive components on varying pallet configurations from different suppliers, or in distribution centers handling a mixed pallet assortment, fork positioners reduce cycle time and improve pallet entry accuracy. They are commonly paired with sideshifters as a combined unit.

Capacity ImpactModerate - 200 to 500 lbs
Cost Range$1,500 to $4,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts, reach trucks
Best For Operations handling multiple pallet sizes, high-cycle environments where manual fork repositioning creates workflow interruptions, and facilities concerned about operator ergonomics from repeated manual adjustments.
03
Rotator
360-degree fork rotation for dumping, inverting, and positioning loads that cannot be handled upright

A rotator allows the carriage and forks to rotate a full 360 degrees hydraulically. This enables operations that are impossible with standard forks: dumping bulk containers into hoppers or processing equipment, inverting molds or dies, rotating loads for quality inspection, or emptying containers without manual handling. Rotators are a specialized attachment used in specific manufacturing processes rather than general warehouse work.

In the Upstate SC manufacturing corridor, rotators appear most frequently in food processing operations, plastics manufacturing, foundry and metalworking environments, and automotive component manufacturing where specific loads need to be oriented or emptied during the production process. The weight of a rotator is significant and capacity derating must be carefully calculated.

Capacity ImpactSignificant - 500 to 1,500 lbs
Cost Range$3,500 to $10,000+
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts
Best For Manufacturing operations that dump, invert, or reorient loads as part of a production process. Not a general warehouse attachment - the application needs to specifically require rotation to justify the cost and capacity impact.
04
Carton Clamp
Padded arms that clamp and lift cartons, appliances, and boxed goods without a pallet

A carton clamp replaces forks with two large padded arms that squeeze and lift loads from the sides rather than from beneath. This eliminates the need for a pallet entirely, which matters in operations receiving or shipping goods that are not palletized - appliances, furniture, large cartons, and boxed goods that arrive floor-loaded in containers or trailers. Carton clamps are standard equipment in appliance distribution and consumer goods warehousing.

For Upstate SC operations that receive floor-loaded containers - increasingly common in import distribution near the SC Inland Port in Greer - a carton clamp can dramatically reduce the manual labor required to unload and stage goods. What previously required a team of workers hand-bombing product can be handled by a single operator in a fraction of the time.

Capacity ImpactModerate to significant
Cost Range$4,000 to $12,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts
Best For Appliance distribution, consumer goods warehousing, operations receiving floor-loaded containers, and any facility handling large cartons or boxed goods that are not palletized.
05
Push-Pull Attachment
Handles slip-sheet loads without pallets - standard in operations looking to eliminate pallet cost and weight

A push-pull attachment handles loads on slip sheets - thin plastic or fiber sheets - rather than wooden pallets. The attachment grips the lip of the slip sheet, pulls the load onto a plateback, transports it, and pushes it off at the destination. Slip sheet handling eliminates pallet cost, reduces load weight, and allows more product per container since pallets add several inches of height per layer.

Push-pull attachments are particularly common in food and beverage distribution, import/export operations, and retail distribution where the elimination of pallet return logistics creates meaningful cost savings. Several of the larger distribution facilities near the SC Inland Port in Greer run push-pull operations specifically to maximize container utilization on high-volume import lanes.

Capacity ImpactModerate
Cost Range$5,000 to $14,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts
Best For Food and beverage distribution, import operations at or near the SC Inland Port, retail distribution, and any operation where eliminating pallet cost and return logistics creates meaningful savings at volume.
06
Paper Roll Clamp
Curved arms designed to handle paper rolls, film rolls, and large cylindrical loads safely

A paper roll clamp uses large curved arms that conform to the circumference of a roll to grip and lift without damaging the product. Designed originally for the paper and printing industry, roll clamps are used across a range of industries wherever large cylindrical rolls of material need to be handled - paper, film, textiles, wire, cable, and similar products. The clamp can be oriented horizontally or vertically depending on how the roll needs to be picked and placed.

In the Upstate SC market, roll clamps appear at printing operations, packaging manufacturers, film and stretch wrap distributors, and textile operations in Spartanburg and Anderson counties handling rolls of fabric or fiber. Matching the clamp arm radius to the roll diameter range being handled is critical - a clamp sized for large rolls will not grip small rolls safely.

Capacity ImpactModerate to significant
Cost Range$4,500 to $14,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts
Best For Paper, printing, film, textile, wire, and cable operations handling large cylindrical rolls. Roll diameter range must be matched to clamp specifications before ordering.
07
Drum Handler
Mechanical or hydraulic attachment for picking, transporting, and positioning drums safely

Drum handlers come in several configurations - jaw-style units that grip the chime of the drum, parrot beak designs that hook under the drum rim, and more complex hydraulic units that can tilt and pour. All are designed to handle drums more safely and efficiently than improvising with standard forks, which is both a safety risk and a common OSHA finding when operations handle drums without proper equipment.

Chemical distributors, lubricant suppliers, food processing operations, and automotive manufacturing facilities in the Upstate SC market all regularly handle 55-gallon drums as part of their operation. For facilities moving more than a handful of drums per shift, a proper drum handler attachment pays for itself quickly in reduced injury risk and faster handling times.

Capacity ImpactMinor to moderate
Cost Range$500 to $5,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts
Best For Chemical, lubricant, food processing, and manufacturing operations that regularly handle 55-gallon or similar drums as part of their material flow.
08
Bale Clamp
Large hinged arms for handling compressed bales of fiber, paper, cardboard, and recycled materials

A bale clamp uses two large hinged arms to squeeze and lift compressed bales of material - cardboard, paper, plastic film, cotton, synthetic fiber, and similar materials. The clamp exerts significant clamping force to grip the compressed bale securely during transport. Bale clamps are standard equipment in recycling operations, paper mills, textile manufacturing, and any facility processing or moving compressed bulk material.

The Upstate SC textile corridor in Spartanburg and Cherokee counties includes facilities that handle compressed fiber bales as primary material input. Recycling and waste management operations throughout the Greenville metro area also regularly use bale clamps. Clamping pressure must be matched to the material being handled - too little pressure and the bale slips, too much and it damages the product.

Capacity ImpactSignificant
Cost Range$5,000 to $15,000
Common OnCounterbalance forklifts, high-capacity units
Best For Recycling, paper processing, textile manufacturing, and any operation that handles compressed bales of fiber, cardboard, plastic, or similar bulk materials.

Hydraulic System Requirements

Most attachments require additional hydraulic circuits beyond the standard lift and tilt functions on a basic forklift. A sideshifter typically requires one additional circuit. A fork positioner requires one. A rotator, clamp, or push-pull may require two or more. Before specifying an attachment, confirm that the forklift it will be mounted on has the available hydraulic circuits and sufficient hydraulic flow rate to operate the attachment properly.

Retrofitting additional hydraulic circuits onto an existing forklift is possible but adds cost and complexity. For operations planning a new purchase or lease with attachments in mind, specifying the required circuits at the time of order is significantly cheaper than adding them later.

Choosing the Right Attachment

Work through these questions before specifying any attachment:

QuestionWhy It Matters
What is the load type and how is it currently handled?Defines which attachment category applies and whether the current method creates inefficiency or risk
What are the load dimensions and weight?Determines attachment size requirements and triggers capacity derating calculation
How many cycles per shift does this application require?Low-cycle applications may not justify attachment cost - manual methods may be more practical
Does the forklift have the required hydraulic circuits?Missing circuits require retrofit or a different base unit
What is the derated capacity with the attachment installed?Must be verified before operation - OSHA requires nameplate update
Is the attachment going on a purchased unit or a rental?Attachments on rental units require provider approval and may affect rental terms
What training do operators need for this attachment?OSHA requires operator training on the specific truck type and attachment before unsupervised operation

Attachments on Rental Units

If you are renting a forklift and need an attachment, there are two paths. The first is to rent a unit that already has the attachment installed - many rental providers carry units with sideshifters and fork positioners as standard configuration, and some carry units with clamps for specific applications. The second is to source the attachment separately and mount it on the rental unit, which requires explicit approval from the rental provider and should be documented in the rental agreement.

Mounting an attachment on a rental unit without provider approval, operating the unit at the unmodified capacity rating with the attachment in place, or failing to update the nameplate are all OSHA violations that fall on the employer - not the rental provider. Confirm attachment plans with your provider before the unit arrives.
